Arch will be a first-round pick because of his name, not his game.

Just like Bronny James.

Tyler Lengyel · November 16, 2026 · 9 min read

Read the original on X ↗

Remember during the 2023-24 NCAA Men’s College Basketball season when Bronny James wasn’t playing as a starter before declaring for the draft, forgoing his sophomore season? Ya, me either, because it wasn’t something anyone in the world talked about, let alone anyone in Division 1 basketball. The only reason he got the attention he did was because of his father, LeBron James, who’s a top 10 NBA player of all time. To give you some context, Bronny James started 6 out of the 25 games he played in on a team that ended the season going 15-18 while not even making the 2024 NCAA March Madness tournament. His stats for the season, on just his team alone, were eighth in scoring and fourth in assists, and he shot 36.6% from the field and 26.7% from three. Yikes.

Now, if I gave you all this information and you were a decision maker in the front office of an NBA team, you’d laugh at anyone telling you that you should take him in the NBA draft. Hell, anyone with common sense would tell you that this kid needs to go back for his sophomore year, at least, and develop more as a player. That is, unless you have a dad who some consider to be one of the greatest players of all time, which is exactly what happened. Bronny James decided to enter the draft with his horrific stats from his freshman year at the University of Spoiled Children, and he was drafted in the second round, at pick 55, out of a total of 58 picks that were made. The difference between the NBA and the NFL when it comes to the draft is pretty substantial: the NBA has only 2 rounds, with the NFL having 7. I mention this to show how difficult it is to make it to the point of being drafted in the NBA, and in my opinion, Bronny James stole a spot from someone else not because of his skill, but because of who his daddy is, and if you disagree, you’re wrong. Case in point, LeBron James was playing for the Los Angeles Lakers at this time, and that’s exactly who drafted his son.

Listen, I get it, it’s cool to see a father play with his son at the highest level, but it’s not nearly as cool when you realize that other players, who were better than Bronny James, lost out because they didn’t have a father in the same position. So how did this all shake out for the Lakers organization? Well, LeBron just left for another team, again, leaving Bronny in LA, where he’s played in 42.1% of the games since being drafted, averaged 7.6 minutes per game, 2.6 points per game, while shooting 37.6% from the field. Oh, and after this 2026-27 season is over, he’ll have stolen, I’m sorry, earned $5,408,801 from an organization that should’ve told LeBron to shove it a long time ago.

Anywayyyyys, let’s talk about someone whose family name I have more respect for, the Mannings. Watching this weekend’s Texas vs. LSU game made me realize that nepo babies are real, and Arch could end up being another prime example. The definition of a nepotism baby, for those that don’t know, is: a person—most commonly in the entertainment, fashion, or media industries—who is perceived to benefit from their parent’s fame, wealth, social capital, or industry connections to launch or advance their own career. Like Bronny James being drafted after proving in no way, shape, or form that he’s an NBA-caliber player. But I digress; I’m going to continue to tell you my prediction about what I believe will happen with Arch Manning’s future.

Entering the University of Texas in 2023 with as much hype as you’d expect when you’re related to two individuals who both played at the highest level in Peyton and Eli Manning, Arch still rode the bench the majority of his first two seasons, which is respectable when you think about how many coaches and teams would’ve felt the pressure to throw him in right away. This shows you that Arch, for two seasons, had the ability to learn the system under Texas’ head coach Steve Sarkisian, offensive coordinator Kyle Flood, and quarterback coach AJ Milwee, who are still there today running the same system. The reason I mention this is because in college sports, and college football especially, you see coaches jump around more than frogs in a rainstorm. The fact that Arch has had stability during his career at Texas would make one think that he should be hitting his stride and dominating defenses in whatever environment he’s thrown into, but from my point of view, that’s far from the case, and his stats show it.

Going into his third year at Texas in 2025, Arch became the starting QB after Quinn Ewers left for the draft, where he was drafted in the seventh round. Ewers was a good quarterback for Texas; he wasn’t amazing by any stretch of the imagination, but he went in and did his job at a high level. During the 2023 season, he was 15th in passing yards, tied for 37th in passing TDs, 10th in completion percentage, and 17th in passing efficiency. During this season, he led Texas as the No. 3 seed in the four-team College Football Playoff, where they lost to Washington 37-31, ending with a 12-2 record for the season. Then he came back for his final season in 2024, where he finished 14th in the nation in passing yards, tied for 6th in passing TDs, 26th in completion percentage, and 25th in passing efficiency. In his final NCAA season, he led Texas to another College Football Playoff semifinal in the newly structured twelve-team playoff. Texas entered this playoff as the No. 5 overall seed, beating Clemson in the first round, Arizona State in the Peach Bowl quarterfinal, and then ultimately losing to Ohio State in the semifinal, finishing the season with an overall 13-3 record. These were all moments when Arch had the ability to learn, grow, and become the QB everyone in Texas hoped he’d be for the 2025 season.

After making it to the CFP in both the 2023 and 2024 seasons with their former QB Quinn Ewers, Texas failed to make the playoff in the 2025 season, Arch’s first season as the starting QB. At the end of the year, Arch was ranked 27th in total passing yards, tied for 17th in passing TDs, tied for 81st in completion percentage, and 42nd in passing efficiency, which led Texas to a 9-3 regular season record and to miss the CFP as the 13th seed. They went on to beat No. 18 Michigan 41-27 in the Citrus Bowl, where, to his credit, he did win the game’s MVP award. Regardless of this final victory, most would consider it a very underwhelming season for the University of Texas football team.

Now we enter the current 2026 season with Texas starting out as No. 5 in the AP poll. Many would expect to see much improvement from Arch and the Texas offense compared to the previous ’25 season, but it wasn’t even a month ago when Texas went into a home game against Ole Miss where analysts were talking about if Arch might get benched because of his lackluster performances leading up to that game, losing to Oklahoma and Florida in back-to-back games, respectively. (Oklahoma is currently unranked; Florida is currently ranked 25 after beating Kentucky this past weekend.) In the Red River Showdown against OU, Arch had a 61.3% completion percentage, 260 passing yards, 3 passing TDs, and 2 interceptions, losing the game 27-24 on a last-minute FG by OU. The next weekend, playing at home against Florida, Arch had a 70% completion percentage, 173 passing yards, 1 passing TD, and no interceptions, losing the game 27-24. You can see why some experts would question if Arch was the answer going forward, and even though they go on to win their next 4 games, I’m here to tell you, it hasn’t been because of Arch Manning.

On October 24th, in another home game for Texas against Ole Miss, Arch had a 60% completion percentage, 212 passing yards, 1 passing TD, and 2 interceptions. They went on to beat Ole Miss 34-27 in a game that Ole Miss could’ve easily pulled out. The following week, another home game, Texas played Mississippi State, who lost their starting QB before this game to season-ending surgery on his toe. UT went on to win 31-3, and Arch had an 80% completion percentage, 220 passing yards, 3 passing TDs, and 0 interceptions against the 106th-ranked defense and 128th overall passing defense. After this victory, Texas went to Missouri, where people were saying Texas would lose, but won easily 34-20, and Arch had another good performance with a 73.7% completion percentage, 297 passing yards, 3 passing TDs, and back-to-back games with 0 interceptions against a then ranked 37th defense and 92nd ranked passing defense. Then we come to this past weekend, which was the first game I watched from beginning to end with Arch Manning as the starting QB, and as you can probably tell, I was very unimpressed. Not just by his play, but by his demeanor. With Arch as a starting QB for a top 10 team in the nation with the last name Manning, I expected to see more emotion, but saw little to none. He always seemed to be smiling or unwilling to get frustrated after throwing an interception when pictured on the bench after the fact, throwing his hair back, unfazed. It was frustrating to watch because he shows emotion and talks that shit when he scores a TD, but seems completely fine after throwing an interception that could cost his team the game. It reminds me of a kid with a silver spoon up his ass, but I digress. Texas went on to win the game with 2 seconds left on a swing play to a running back, which was a great play call to take the lead and win 22-21. Arch had a 54.3% completion percentage, 174 passing yards, 1 passing TD, and 3 interceptions. The only TD came on the last play of the game, given this was the 6th ranked defense and 17th ranked passing defense going into the game.

For all the hype, Arch is currently 36th in the nation in passing yards, tied for 19th in passing TDs, tied for 51st in completion percentage, and 43rd in passing efficiency. If the season were to end today, Arch would be worse statistically than he was in the 2025 season, but the difference is that this season they seem to have a guaranteed spot in the CFP, where, in my opinion, his true colors will be shown. You might call me a hater, but truth be told, I’ve been rooting for Arch Manning; I bought into the hype, but what he’s shown me is a guy that doesn’t care about winning, regardless of how you view him jumping up and down after the winning TD against LSU. One thing I can say I’m certain of, though, is that he will be a first-round draft pick regardless of how his collegiate career finishes, not because of how he performed, but because of the name on the back of his jersey. This, from where I sit, is where things need to change, because if they don’t, better players will get passed up on, the rich will get richer, and we’ll all get cheated out of better sports and stories because of it.

Thanks for reading,

Tyler Lengyel
