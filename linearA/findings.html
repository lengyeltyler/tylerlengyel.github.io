Three clues from Linear A

Imagine finding a list written in a language you cannot read. What could you learn from a drawing, the order of the entries or even one disputed mark?

What did someone count 3,000 of?

On tablet HT 31, the writer drew a small cup beside PA-TA-QE and 3,000. A similar cup sits beside a different word and 300. PA-TA-QE may name a kind of cup. The drawing alone cannot tell us what made these cups different. See the cup drawings on HT 31.

Why do two labels keep turning up together?

On both sides of tablet HT 95, DA-ME comes just before MI-NU-TE. A second tablet, HT 86, puts them in the same order. That makes three lists on two pieces of clay.

Perhaps scribes kept a standard list, like a class roster. They could also have copied from an earlier record. If the first idea is right, we would expect the same order on another independently written list. Each label also appears on its own elsewhere. We still do not know what either one means.

Was Jasumatu a person?

On an object from Syme known as SY Za 2, four signs in a row read JA-SU-MA-TU. People sometimes write these together as “Jasumatu.” The puzzle is the next mark.

Some researchers see an olive sign. On that reading, Jasumatu might be someone offering olives. Others read the mark as another sound, RE. That gives JA-SU-MA-TU-RE, and the clue about olives disappears. A third idea connects SU-MA to a place and suggests the phrase could mean someone from Suma. We cannot yet identify a person or a place with confidence.
